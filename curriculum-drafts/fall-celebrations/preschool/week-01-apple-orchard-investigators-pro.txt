WEEKLY_OVERVIEW:
Preschool children become apple orchard investigators as they explore how apples grow, compare apple varieties, investigate the parts of an apple, practice counting and measuring, create apple-inspired art, and work together in orchard dramatic play. The week is intentionally play-based and combines sensory investigation, early science, language, math, music, movement, fine-motor work, and cooperative pretend play. Children are encouraged to make predictions, test ideas, describe what they notice, represent discoveries through drawing and building, and revisit their thinking throughout the week. The teacher documents children’s language, problem-solving, collaboration, and emerging understanding of plant growth while allowing multiple ways to participate.

LEARNING_OBJECTIVES:
Children will describe apples using color, size, texture, smell, taste, and shape words.
Children will identify and name basic parts of an apple, including skin, flesh, stem, seeds, and core.
Children will compare, sort, count, measure, and create simple patterns with apple-related materials.
Children will sequence a simple apple life cycle from seed to tree to blossom to fruit.
Children will ask questions, make predictions, test ideas, and communicate observations.
Children will strengthen fine-motor control through grasping, transferring, drawing, cutting, stamping, and manipulating play materials.
Children will strengthen gross-motor coordination through balancing, carrying, reaching, jumping, and orchard movement games.
Children will use new vocabulary in conversation, storytelling, dramatic play, and teacher-guided discussion.
Children will cooperate, take turns, negotiate roles, and contribute ideas during shared investigations and pretend play.
Children will express ideas creatively through process art, music, movement, construction, and storytelling.

FAMILY_CONNECTION:
Invite families to look for apples at home, in a store, or outdoors and talk about color, size, smell, texture, or favorite ways to eat them. Families may send a photo, drawing, empty clean apple package, favorite apple recipe, or short story about an apple tradition. Make participation optional and provide classroom alternatives so every child can contribute. At the end of the week, send home one child observation or quote about the apple investigation and a simple suggestion to compare two fruits together.

OBSERVATION_OPPORTUNITIES:
Document descriptive vocabulary used during sensory exploration and tasting.
Note whether children sort by one attribute, change sorting rules, or explain their reasoning.
Observe one-to-one correspondence and counting accuracy during basket and seed games.
Record strategies children use to measure circumference, height, weight, or distance.
Listen for predictions before cutting, tasting, floating, building, or testing.
Notice whether children revise an idea after seeing new evidence.
Observe fine-motor control with tongs, drawing tools, scissors, dough tools, and stamps.
Document cooperation, role negotiation, turn-taking, and problem-solving in orchard dramatic play.
Collect drawings, dictated explanations, photographs, and child quotes for portfolios.
Notice children who communicate through gesture, signs, pictures, home language, or assistive communication as valid demonstrations of learning.

ADAPTATIONS:
Offer whole apples, large felt apples, photographs, or sealed sensory bags for children who should not handle small seeds or wet food textures.
Provide large-handled tongs, adaptive scissors, thick crayons, non-slip mats, and pre-cut materials for varied motor needs.
Use visual sequence cards, first-then language, modeled gestures, and short one-step directions before combining steps.
Allow children to observe before touching, use tools instead of hands, wear gloves, or choose a dry alternative during sensory and food experiences.
Provide seated, standing, and floor-level participation choices.
Use picture vocabulary cards and repeat key words in simple sentences; honor home-language responses and gestures.
Pair children intentionally for peer modeling without requiring verbal participation.
Reduce the number of choices or materials when a child benefits from a simpler workspace.
Extend learning for children ready for more by inviting them to record numerals, invent sorting rules, compare multiple measurements, create labels, or explain cause-and-effect ideas.
Never require tasting; offer smelling, looking, drawing, or comparing as equal participation options.

DAILY_THEME:
Meet the Apple Investigators: Using Our Senses

DAILY_OBJECTIVES:
Children will investigate apples using sight, touch, smell, and sound.
Children will use descriptive words to compare two or more apples.
Children will practice asking questions and recording observations.
Children will cooperate while sharing tools and materials.

DAILY_VOCABULARY:
investigate, observe, smooth, bumpy, shiny, dull, firm, soft, crisp, juicy, sweet, tart

DAILY_MATERIALS:
Several washed apples in different colors and sizes; trays; magnifying glasses; chart paper; markers; clipboards; crayons; paper; baskets; sensory description picture cards; optional allergy-safe apple slices prepared according to program policy; cups and napkins.

DAILY_LEARNING_DOMAINS:
Social Emotional, Language & Literacy, Science, Physical Development, Creative Arts

DAILY_BOOKS:
Apples | Gail Gibbons | Read selected introductory pages and pause for predictions.

DAILY_SONGS:
Way Up High in the Apple Tree | Traditional fingerplay | Use slow motions and count imaginary apples.

CIRCLE_TIME:
Reveal a covered basket and invite children to predict what might be inside using clues such as round, grows on a tree, and can be red, green, or yellow.
Introduce the word investigator and explain that investigators look closely, ask questions, and share what they notice.
Pass one whole apple for a careful observation while children offer describing words; record all communication forms on chart paper.
Read selected pages from Apples by Gail Gibbons and compare the book photographs with the real apples.
Sing Way Up High in the Apple Tree with reaching, shaking, and gentle falling motions.
Explain that tasting later is optional and that looking, smelling, touching with a tool, or drawing are also ways to investigate.

TRANSITIONS:
Invite children to move to centers by choosing an apple word or pointing to a picture card.
Use the chant, “Red, green, yellow too, apple investigators know what to do.”
Ask children to pretend to carry a tiny apple, medium apple, or giant apple while walking safely.

OUTDOOR_PLAY:
Take an apple-color hunt around the playground or outdoor area. Children search for natural or classroom-safe objects that match red, green, yellow, or brown apple colors without picking living plants. Add reaching high like orchard pickers, crouching low to inspect the ground, and carrying pretend baskets back to a meeting spot.

DAILY_FAMILY_CONNECTION:
Ask children whether their families eat apples whole, sliced, cooked, or in another way. Record responses without ranking one experience over another.

DAILY_OBSERVATIONS:
Words or communication methods used to describe apples.
Willingness to compare, ask questions, or revisit an observation.
Tool-sharing and turn-taking during investigation.
Sensory preferences and self-advocacy choices.

DAILY_ADAPTATIONS:
Provide a sealed bag containing an apple slice for visual exploration without direct contact.
Offer one apple at a time and a quiet investigation space.
Use yes/no, choice-of-two, or picture prompts instead of open-ended verbal questions when helpful.
Place apples on non-slip mats and offer a stand magnifier for children who need hands free.

SAFETY_NOTES:
Check food allergies and program policies before any tasting.
Wash apples and hands before food handling.
Adults cut all firm whole apples unless approved child-safe tools and direct supervision are used.
Seeds and small pieces are choking hazards; supervise closely and follow age-appropriate food preparation guidance.
Do not require tasting or touching.

ACTIVITY_NAME:
Apple Investigation Lab
CATEGORY:
STEM/Discovery
OBJECTIVE:
Children will use multiple senses and simple tools to observe, compare, and describe apples.
DESCRIPTION:
Children rotate through a calm investigation area where they look closely at whole apples, touch or use tools to explore the surface, smell near the stem, listen while an apple is gently tapped, and record discoveries through drawing, marks, dictated words, gestures, or photographs.
LEARNING_DOMAINS:
Science, Language & Literacy, Physical Development, Social Emotional
MATERIALS:
Washed whole apples; trays; magnifying glasses; clipboards; paper; crayons; picture vocabulary cards; optional balance scale.
SETUP:
Place two or three contrasting apples on individual trays. Add magnifiers, paper, and description cards. Keep the number of materials limited enough for focused observation and safe tool use.
TEACHER_ROLE:
Model how to look closely without telling children what they should notice. Accept all forms of communication, write down child words, support tool-sharing, and invite children to compare their current idea with a later observation.
TEACHER_LANGUAGE:
“What do you notice first?”
“How does this apple feel? What makes you say that?”
“What is the same about these apples? What is different?”
“What could we use to look even closer?”
“You may touch it, use a tool, look, or draw. Which way works for you?”
DIRECTIONS:
1. Invite children to choose one apple and observe it before using any tools.
2. Offer a magnifying glass and model moving it slowly over the skin, stem, and blossom end.
3. Encourage children to describe or indicate color, shape, texture, smell, and size.
4. Place a second apple nearby and invite comparison.
5. Let each child record one discovery through drawing, marks, dictation, gesture, or a teacher-taken photo.
6. Add discoveries to a shared “What We Notice About Apples” chart.
LEARNING_GOALS:
Use senses and tools for observation.
Communicate descriptive information.
Compare visible and tactile properties.
Practice sustained attention and shared tool use.
OBSERVATION_OPPORTUNITIES:
Does the child attend to more than one feature?
Does the child use or understand comparison words?
How does the child communicate sensory preferences?
Does the child use the magnifier purposefully?
VOCABULARY:
investigate, observe, smooth, bumpy, shiny, firm, stem, skin
EXTENSIONS:
Invite children to draw the apple from two different angles.
Add a scale and ask which apple might be heavier before testing.
Photograph close-up skin patterns and match photos to the correct apple.
ADAPTATIONS:
Use a stand magnifier, large apple photographs, or a sealed sensory bag.
Offer a simple two-column same/different board.
Provide extra wait time and sentence starters such as “I notice…”
SAFETY_NOTES:
Keep whole apples on trays so they do not roll into walkways. Supervise magnifier use and sanitize shared materials as required.
AGE_MODIFICATIONS:
For younger preschoolers, focus on choosing between two describing words. For older preschoolers, invite detailed comparison sentences, labeled drawings, or a question they want to investigate later.

ACTIVITY_NAME:
Apple Taste Test Without Pressure
CATEGORY:
Sensory Play
OBJECTIVE:
Children will compare optional samples and communicate preferences using respectful language.
DESCRIPTION:
Children may taste small allergy-safe pieces of different apple varieties or participate by smelling, looking, touching with a utensil, or matching sample colors. The focus is investigation rather than finishing food or choosing a “best” apple.
LEARNING_DOMAINS:
Science, Language & Literacy, Social Emotional
MATERIALS:
Two or three approved apple varieties; adult cutting tools; small cups or plates; napkins; water; preference chart; picture choice cards.
SETUP:
Prepare bite-size pieces according to program food-safety and choking-prevention rules. Label samples with simple symbols or colors. Set out non-tasting participation choices.
TEACHER_ROLE:
Protect children’s right to decline, model neutral sensory language, prevent negative comments about others’ preferences, and record responses without turning the activity into a competition.
TEACHER_LANGUAGE:
“You decide whether to taste, smell, look, or pass.”
“What do you notice about this sample?”
“Your choice can be different from your friend’s choice.”
“Is it crisp, soft, juicy, sweet, tart, or something else?”
DIRECTIONS:
1. Review handwashing and explain all participation choices.
2. Show the whole apples before revealing prepared pieces.
3. Invite children to investigate one sample at a time.
4. Pause for children to communicate observations in words, pictures, gestures, or signs.
5. Record preferences on a class chart using names, photos, or tally marks only with appropriate privacy practices.
6. Compare the chart and emphasize that different preferences are welcome.
LEARNING_GOALS:
Use sensory vocabulary.
Make and communicate a choice.
Respect different preferences.
Notice similarities and differences among varieties.
OBSERVATION_OPPORTUNITIES:
How does the child communicate consent or refusal?
Which sensory words does the child use or understand?
Can the child compare two samples?
Does the child respond respectfully to peers’ choices?
VOCABULARY:
taste, smell, crisp, juicy, sweet, tart, prefer, same, different
EXTENSIONS:
Graph class responses by sample color or variety.
Invite children to dictate a sentence for an apple tasting book.
Compare apple texture with another familiar fruit on a later day.
ADAPTATIONS:
Offer only visual comparison, sealed samples, utensils, gloves, or a familiar food alternative.
Use picture cards for “try,” “smell,” “look,” and “no thank you.”
SAFETY_NOTES:
Confirm allergies, permissions, sanitation, food storage, and choking-prevention procedures. Adults prepare firm fruit as required. Never pressure a child to eat.
AGE_MODIFICATIONS:
Younger preschoolers may choose between simple picture words such as sweet/tart or crunchy/soft. Older preschoolers may explain a preference with evidence and contribute to a tally graph.

DAILY_THEME:
Apple Math: Sorting, Counting, and Measuring

DAILY_OBJECTIVES:
Children will sort apples or apple models by observable attributes.
Children will count with one-to-one correspondence.
Children will compare weight, height, and circumference using informal tools.
Children will explain or demonstrate a sorting or measuring strategy.

DAILY_VOCABULARY:
sort, group, count, compare, measure, heavier, lighter, equal, taller, shorter, around, circumference, pattern

DAILY_MATERIALS:
Whole apples or durable apple models; baskets; sorting hoops; numeral and dot cards; balance scale; linking cubes; yarn; scissors for adult use; chart paper; felt apples; pattern strips; tongs.

DAILY_LEARNING_DOMAINS:
Math, Science, Language & Literacy, Physical Development, Social Emotional

DAILY_BOOKS:
Ten Apples Up On Top! | Dr. Seuss writing as Theo. LeSieg, illustrated by Roy McKie | Pause to count and predict what comes next.

DAILY_SONGS:
Ten Red Apples | Traditional counting chant | Remove one felt apple each verse.

CIRCLE_TIME:
Read Ten Apples Up On Top! and invite children to count, show quantities with fingers, and predict the next number.
Display three apples and ask children for different ways they could be grouped.
Model that one collection can be sorted more than one way: by color, size, presence of a stem, or another child-generated rule.
Introduce the balance scale and demonstrate prediction before testing.
Create a quick body pattern such as reach, crouch, reach, crouch to connect patterns with movement.

TRANSITIONS:
Give each child a dot card and invite them to take that many careful steps to the next area.
Call children by a sorting attribute such as “friends wearing red” while ensuring every child is included through multiple neutral categories.
Repeat an apple movement pattern for children to copy while waiting.

OUTDOOR_PLAY:
Set up an “orchard delivery path” with cones and baskets. Children carry one to five beanbag apples, count them into a basket, and compare whether the basket feels lighter or heavier after each delivery. Provide short and long paths and seated transfer options.

DAILY_FAMILY_CONNECTION:
Invite children to notice how apples or other groceries are grouped or counted at home or in a store.

DAILY_OBSERVATIONS:
Sorting rule selected and whether the child can maintain it.
Counting accuracy and one-to-one correspondence.
Use of measurement language and prediction.
Persistence when a strategy does not work immediately.

DAILY_ADAPTATIONS:
Use large felt apples instead of real apples.
Limit sorting to two clear groups before adding complexity.
Provide numeral-dot matching cards.
Offer a tabletop balance alternative and seated delivery game.

SAFETY_NOTES:
Prevent apples from rolling onto the floor.
Supervise balance-scale pieces, yarn, tongs, and movement paths.
Use only lightweight objects for carrying and balancing.

ACTIVITY_NAME:
Apple Sorting Challenge
CATEGORY:
Math
OBJECTIVE:
Children will create, test, and explain ways to sort a collection of apples or apple models.
DESCRIPTION:
Children explore a mixed collection and decide how items belong together. After completing one sort, they are invited to mix the collection and invent a new rule, showing that mathematicians can organize the same materials in different ways.
LEARNING_DOMAINS:
Math, Science, Language & Literacy, Social Emotional
MATERIALS:
Whole apples or apple models in varied colors and sizes; baskets or hoops; attribute picture cards; clipboards optional.
SETUP:
Place a manageable mixed collection in the center with two or three empty baskets. Keep extra attribute cards nearby rather than directing the first sort.
TEACHER_ROLE:
Observe before suggesting a rule, invite children to explain their thinking, help resolve disagreements through testing, and document child-created categories.
TEACHER_LANGUAGE:
“How could these apples belong in groups?”
“What is your rule?”
“Where would this apple go? How do you know?”
“Could we sort the same apples a different way?”
“What should we do with an apple that fits both groups?”
DIRECTIONS:
1. Invite children to examine the full collection.
2. Ask them to propose or demonstrate a way to group the apples.
3. Place items one at a time while children explain or indicate the rule.
4. Check the completed groups together for consistency.
5. Mix the collection and invite a second sorting rule.
6. Photograph or sketch both arrangements for comparison.
LEARNING_GOALS:
Classify by observable attributes.
Maintain and communicate a rule.
Recognize multiple valid solutions.
Collaborate and revise ideas.
OBSERVATION_OPPORTUNITIES:
Does the child sort by color, size, shape, or another feature?
Can the child explain placement verbally or nonverbally?
Does the child notice an item that does not fit the rule?
Can the child accept or test a peer’s idea?
VOCABULARY:
sort, group, rule, attribute, same, different, belongs
EXTENSIONS:
Use a Venn-style overlapping hoop sort.
Graph the number in each group.
Invite children to invent a mystery rule for peers to discover.
ADAPTATIONS:
Begin with two highly contrasting groups.
Use tactile symbols or large photographs.
Allow the child to point while an adult moves materials.
SAFETY_NOTES:
Use stable baskets and keep rolling items centered on a rug or tray. Supervise any small counters.
AGE_MODIFICATIONS:
Younger preschoolers sort by one obvious attribute. Older preschoolers may use two attributes, create overlapping groups, or record quantities.

ACTIVITY_NAME:
How Big Is an Apple?
CATEGORY:
STEM/Discovery
OBJECTIVE:
Children will use informal measurement tools to compare apple height, circumference, and weight.
DESCRIPTION:
Children rotate among measurement choices: stacking cubes beside an apple, wrapping yarn around its widest part, and comparing two apples on a balance scale. They predict before testing and record results with drawings, numerals, or teacher dictation.
LEARNING_DOMAINS:
Math, Science, Language & Literacy, Physical Development
MATERIALS:
Apples; linking cubes; yarn; balance scale; trays; recording sheet; crayons; comparison word cards.
SETUP:
Create three clearly marked spaces for height, around, and weight. Pre-cut several long yarn pieces or have an adult cut yarn after wrapping.
TEACHER_ROLE:
Model aligning tools carefully, emphasize comparison rather than exact standard units, and ask children to explain what the tool shows.
TEACHER_LANGUAGE:
“Which apple do you predict will be heavier?”
“Where should our cubes start?”
“How can the yarn help us find the distance around?”
“Did the biggest-looking apple weigh the most?”
“What evidence do you have?”
DIRECTIONS:
1. Choose two apples and make a prediction about one measurement.
2. Stack cubes from the table to the top of each apple and compare.
3. Wrap yarn once around the widest part and compare yarn lengths.
4. Place one apple on each side of the balance scale.
5. Record one result through drawing, numeral, photo, or dictation.
6. Share a result and discuss whether the prediction matched the evidence.
LEARNING_GOALS:
Use tools purposefully.
Compare measurable attributes.
Connect prediction with evidence.
Use measurement vocabulary.
OBSERVATION_OPPORTUNITIES:
Does the child align cubes consistently?
Can the child interpret which side of the scale is heavier?
Does the child revise a prediction?
Which recording method supports the child best?
VOCABULARY:
measure, height, around, circumference, weight, heavier, lighter, equal, predict
EXTENSIONS:
Order three apples from lightest to heaviest.
Create a class chart of cube heights.
Compare apple circumference with wrist or block circumference without ranking bodies.
ADAPTATIONS:
Use magnetic cubes, larger blocks, or a partner to stabilize materials.
Offer pre-cut yarn choices to match around the apple.
Use a digital photo record instead of drawing.
SAFETY_NOTES:
Supervise yarn and scissors. Use a sturdy balance scale on a low table and prevent apples from falling.
AGE_MODIFICATIONS:
Younger preschoolers compare two objects using heavier/lighter and taller/shorter. Older preschoolers may count units, order three items, and discuss fair measuring procedures.

DAILY_THEME:
Inside an Apple: Parts, Seeds, and Change

DAILY_OBJECTIVES:
Children will identify basic apple parts.
Children will predict what the inside of an apple looks like before cutting.
Children will count and compare seeds.
Children will represent apple parts through art, drawing, or construction.

DAILY_VOCABULARY:
skin, flesh, core, seed, stem, half, whole, inside, outside, slice, predict, evidence

DAILY_MATERIALS:
Whole apples; adult knife; cutting board; document camera or close viewing tray; magnifiers; tweezers; bowls; paper plates; crayons; paint; apple halves for stamping; construction paper; glue; seed counters larger than choking size or paper seed shapes; apple-part labels; play dough.

DAILY_LEARNING_DOMAINS:
Science, Math, Language & Literacy, Creative Arts, Physical Development

DAILY_BOOKS:
The Apple Pie Tree | Zoe Hall, illustrated by Shari Halpern | Connect blossoms, fruit, and seasonal change.

DAILY_SONGS:
Apple Tree Movement Song | Teacher-created | Move from seed to tree to blossom to fruit.

CIRCLE_TIME:
Show a whole apple and invite children to draw in the air or describe what they predict is inside.
Record predictions before cutting.
An adult cuts the apple across the middle and lengthwise so children can compare views.
Name skin, flesh, core, seeds, and stem while allowing children to offer their own words first.
Count visible seeds and discuss why different apples may not have the same number.
Read The Apple Pie Tree and revisit where fruit comes from.

TRANSITIONS:
Invite children to move like an apple seed, root, trunk, branch, blossom, or fruit.
Call out whole or half and let children make a large or small body shape.
Use the phrase, “Scientists predict, then scientists check.”

OUTDOOR_PLAY:
Create an apple life-cycle movement path: curl like a seed, crawl like a root, stand tall like a trunk, stretch arms like branches, flutter fingers like blossoms, and carry a beanbag fruit to the harvest basket.

DAILY_FAMILY_CONNECTION:
Encourage children to tell someone at home one apple part they learned and one question they still have.

DAILY_OBSERVATIONS:
Predictions before cutting and reactions after seeing evidence.
Ability to identify or match apple parts.
Counting strategies used with seeds or paper seed shapes.
Scientific language used during drawing and construction.

DAILY_ADAPTATIONS:
Use a large labeled apple photograph or felt cross-section.
Provide large paper seed pieces instead of real seeds.
Offer play dough or collage as alternatives to wet paint.
Use repeated gestures for inside, outside, half, and whole.

SAFETY_NOTES:
Only adults use sharp knives.
Remove real seeds promptly when they pose a choking risk.
Use washable non-toxic art materials.
Supervise stamping and clean food-based art materials according to sanitation policy.

ACTIVITY_NAME:
Apple Dissection Investigation
CATEGORY:
STEM/Discovery
OBJECTIVE:
Children will observe and identify apple parts while comparing predictions with evidence.
DESCRIPTION:
Children first predict what is inside a whole apple. An adult opens the apple in two directions, and children use magnifiers to investigate the cross-sections, locate parts, count visible seeds, and create a labeled or dictated record.
LEARNING_DOMAINS:
Science, Math, Language & Literacy
MATERIALS:
Whole apple; adult knife; cutting board; tray; magnifiers; tweezers; bowls; apple-part picture labels; paper; crayons.
SETUP:
Place the whole apple and prediction paper at group meeting. Prepare a separate adult cutting space and a tray for the opened apple.
TEACHER_ROLE:
Maintain safe cutting procedures, elicit predictions before revealing the inside, introduce accurate vocabulary after observation, and help children connect evidence to prior ideas.
TEACHER_LANGUAGE:
“What do you predict is under the skin?”
“What changed when we cut it a different direction?”
“Where do you see the core?”
“How could we count the seeds carefully?”
“Did the evidence match your idea? What would you change?”
DIRECTIONS:
1. Examine the whole apple and record individual or group predictions.
2. Have an adult cut one apple lengthwise and another crosswise, or cut one apple in stages.
3. Place pieces on a tray for close observation.
4. Identify skin, flesh, core, stem, blossom end, and seeds using picture labels.
5. Count visible seeds and compare sections.
6. Invite children to draw, label, dictate, or photograph one discovery.
LEARNING_GOALS:
Make and revisit predictions.
Identify basic fruit structures.
Count small sets carefully.
Use evidence to communicate a discovery.
OBSERVATION_OPPORTUNITIES:
What prior knowledge appears in predictions?
Can the child match labels to parts?
How does the child organize seeds for counting?
Does the child notice differences between cross-sections?
VOCABULARY:
skin, flesh, core, seed, stem, cross-section, half, whole, evidence
EXTENSIONS:
Compare an apple cross-section with pear or citrus photographs.
Leave one slice exposed and one covered for a supervised browning observation.
Create a class nonfiction page about apple parts.
ADAPTATIONS:
Use oversized felt parts or a projected photograph.
Place the apple in a sealed clear container after cutting.
Allow pointing or eye gaze to select labels.
SAFETY_NOTES:
Adults control knives and cutting boards. Closely supervise real seeds and dispose of them safely. Follow sanitation procedures for food materials.
AGE_MODIFICATIONS:
Younger preschoolers match two or three basic parts. Older preschoolers may label a diagram, compare two cutting directions, and explain prediction versus evidence.

ACTIVITY_NAME:
Apple Print Process Art
CATEGORY:
Art
OBJECTIVE:
Children will explore shape, symmetry, color mixing, pressure, and repeated printmaking with apple halves.
DESCRIPTION:
Children use apple halves as printing tools on open-ended paper. They may overlap prints, mix apple colors, add stems or leaves, or transform prints into their own designs. The focus is experimentation rather than producing identical crafts.
LEARNING_DOMAINS:
Creative Arts, Physical Development, Science, Language & Literacy
MATERIALS:
Apple halves; washable paint; shallow trays; large paper; smocks; damp cloths; crayons or markers; optional paper stems and leaves.
SETUP:
Secure paper to the table. Pour small amounts of paint into shallow trays and place one apple half per color when possible. Set finished-work drying area nearby.
TEACHER_ROLE:
Model pressing and lifting without prescribing a final product, invite children to notice the star-like center or changing prints, and support cleanup routines.
TEACHER_LANGUAGE:
“What happened when you pressed gently? What about firmly?”
“What shapes do you notice in the print?”
“How are the first and last prints different?”
“What could your apple prints become?”
DIRECTIONS:
1. Look closely at the cut surface of an apple half.
2. Dip or brush a thin layer of paint onto the surface.
3. Press the apple onto paper and lift straight up.
4. Repeat, overlap, rotate, or change colors according to the child’s plan.
5. Add drawing or collage details if desired.
6. Invite the child to title or describe the work.
LEARNING_GOALS:
Experiment with a nontraditional art tool.
Observe cause and effect in printmaking.
Strengthen grasp and controlled pressure.
Represent ideas creatively.
